## About (bio)

I am a Teaching Assistant Professor of English & Comparative Literature at UNC Chapel Hill, where I direct the Digital Literacy and Communications (DLC) Lab and the Critical Game Studies Program. My courses range from horror fiction to tabletop game design, and in most of them students play, dismantle, and build games. I co-host Subject Matter: Table Top, a podcast about board games and the subjects they take up, and design games of my own, as experiments in play as a form of inquiry. I also build small digital tools, from reading editions to local language models, on the principle that understanding how these technologies work clarifies the conditions of our labor.

I trained in literary and cultural studies at Carnegie Mellon University, where my doctoral work drew together media history, digital humanities, and critical university studies. I grew up in California's high Mojave Desert, east of the Sierra Nevada near Death Valley, and spent my undergraduate years as a banana slug at UC Santa Cruz, majoring in American Studies.

## Books and book projects (Research)

- **Working Models: Computation, Labor, and the Procedures of Play**
  - status: In development
  - description: A study of how games model work. The book examines cases across analog and digital games, from the managerial arithmetic of worker placement mechanics, through automation games in which the factory runs without anyone in it, and work simulators in which work itself is all there is, to the self-valorizing financial energetics of historical railroad games, and character creation systems that render the labor of self-invention as a computable object. / The book explores how games make the cultural organization of work legible to us as players — available to be manipulated, optimized, or broken — and asks what follows from the procedural fluency they leave behind.
- **Terminal Degree: Computing and the End of the Public University**
  - status: Manuscript in preparation
  - description: A history of computing and the public university since the Cold War. It traces how universities built the computing industry, reorganized themselves around it, and now face AI tools that threaten to replace the institutions that produced their training data. / Computing, the book argues, has been both the university's terminal achievement and the grounds of its potential termination.
  - press: Johns Hopkins University Press, series in Critical University Studies (under contract)
  - collaborators: Avery J. Wiscomb
- **Re-Tool Kit**
  - status: Digital supplement, launching with the book
  - description: An online companion to *Terminal Degree*, with practical guides and resources for scholars and graduate students putting the book's strategies to work.
  - collaborators: Avery J. Wiscomb
